Chatbot vs AI agent: what is the difference?

A chatbot answers messages: each turn ends with a reply, and a person decides what happens next. An AI agent is given a goal and works towards it, choosing steps, calling tools that read or change other systems, checking the results and continuing until it finishes or stops. ChatGPT is a chatbot when it simply replies, and behaves more like an agent when it searches, runs code or takes actions for you inside a task.

The short version

The difference between a chatbot and an AI agent is not how clever the language is. Both can use the same large language model and sound equally fluent. The difference is who drives the work. A chatbot waits for you after every answer. An agent takes the next step itself, and some of those steps reach outside the conversation, into a calendar, a database, a codebase or a ticketing system.

A useful test is to ask two questions of any product. First, can it do more than one step before it comes back to you? Second, can any of those steps change something in the world? If the answer to both is no, it is a chatbot, however good it is. If both are yes, you are looking at an agent, and it needs to be designed, tested and governed like one.

If you want the background on what makes something agentic in general, what agentic AI is covers the loop and the levels of autonomy. This guide stays on the practical comparison: the same chat window can hide either design, and the choice changes cost, risk and how you build.

What is the difference between AI and a chatbot?

These are not two options to choose between. AI is the technology; a chatbot is one way of presenting it. A chatbot is a product that talks to people through messages. It can be built with no AI at all, using buttons and scripted flows, or with intent classifiers, or with a large language model. Many AI systems, such as a fraud model or a document classifier, have no chat interface at all.

So when someone says "we added AI to our chatbot", the useful follow-up is what the AI actually does. Usually it means the bot now understands free-form questions and writes its answers rather than picking from a script. That is a real improvement, and it is still a chatbot. The ways those designs differ, from scripted flows to language models, are covered in conversational AI vs chatbots.

Is ChatGPT a chatbot or an AI agent?

It depends on what it is doing in a given conversation. When you ask a question and it replies from the model's own knowledge, ChatGPT is acting as a chatbot, a very capable one. When it decides to search the web, run code to analyse a file you uploaded, or work through a multi-step task in a browser, it is showing agent behaviour: it chooses actions, uses tools and reads the results before answering.

Two caveats keep this honest. First, the features change often and differ between plans, so check what the current product offers rather than relying on any article, including this one. Second, even in its most agent-like modes, a general assistant only acts within the tools and permissions its provider gives it. It cannot update your CRM or approve a refund in your systems unless someone builds that connection.

This is where the comparison "AI agent vs ChatGPT" usually ends up. A general assistant is broad and works for one person at a time. A custom agent is narrow: it is built around your tools, your data and your rules, and it can run as part of a business process without someone typing a prompt each time.

One request, handled two ways

Take a customer message: "I was charged twice for my order last week."

A chatbot, even one grounded in your help centre with retrieval, would explain the refund policy, perhaps ask for the order number and tell the customer that the billing team will be in touch. Every fact it gives comes from documents, and the actual work happens later, when a person reads the ticket.

An agent built for the same job would look up the customer's orders, find the two charges, check whether the second one matches the rules for an automatic refund, and either issue it or prepare the case for a person to approve. Then it would tell the customer what happened. The conversation is the same; what goes on behind it is not.

Notice what the agent needed that the chatbot did not: read access to orders and payments, a defined refund action, rules about when it may act alone, and a record of what it did. Those are the real costs of moving from a chatbot to an agent.

What changes when you build one

From the outside the two can look identical. Under the surface, an agent adds several things a chatbot can do without:

  • Tools with clear contracts. Each action the agent can take is a function with typed inputs, validation and limited permissions. Tool calling for AI agents explains how the model chooses and calls them.
  • State that lasts across steps. The agent has to remember what it has tried and what it found within a task, not just the chat history.
  • Stopping rules. A step limit, a time limit and a defined finished state, so that a confused agent cannot loop forever or keep spending.
  • Approval points. Actions that move money, send messages or delete data usually wait for a person. Human-in-the-loop approval shows how to place them.
  • Logs of every action. When an agent does something unexpected, you need to see each decision and tool call, not only the final message.

A chatbot can still have some of these, such as handing over to a person. But it does not need them to be safe, because its worst outcome is a wrong answer rather than a wrong action.

Risk, cost and testing

Because an agent acts, its failures are different in kind. A chatbot that misunderstands a question gives a poor answer that a person can ignore. An agent that misunderstands a goal may call the wrong tool with the wrong arguments. Prompt injection, where text in an email or web page tries to steer the model, matters far more when the model can act on what it reads. AI agent security risks goes through these in detail.

Agents also tend to cost more per request, because each step can mean another model call, and they take longer to answer. Testing is harder: instead of checking single replies, you check whole runs, including which tools were called and in what order. None of this is a reason to avoid agents, but it is a reason not to build one where a chatbot would do.

How to tell what a product really is

Vendors use "agent" loosely, so ask concrete questions:

  • Which systems can it read from, and which can it change?
  • Can it take more than one action in a row without a person in between?
  • What stops it, and what happens when a tool fails?
  • Which actions need approval, and who gives it?
  • Can you see a log of every step it took for a given request?

If the honest answers are "it reads our help centre" and "it replies once", you are buying a chatbot. That may be exactly what you need.

Which one do you need?

Choose a chatbot when people mainly need answers: questions about policies, products or documentation, where a person or an existing process does the work afterwards. Choose an agent when the value lies in completing tasks that today need someone to click through several systems, and when you can define those actions, their limits and their approvals clearly. Many good systems start as a chatbot, then gain one or two well-guarded actions once the answers are reliable. When not to use AI agents is worth reading before you commit to the larger build.

Frequently asked questions

Is ChatGPT a chatbot or an AI agent?

Both, depending on the task. When it simply replies, it works as a chatbot. When it searches, runs code or carries out steps with tools before answering, it shows agent behaviour, limited to the tools and permissions its provider offers.

What is the difference between AI and a chatbot?

AI is the underlying technology, while a chatbot is a product that talks to people through messages. A chatbot may use no AI, simple intent detection or a large language model, and many AI systems have no chat interface at all.

Can a chatbot become an AI agent?

Yes. Giving a chatbot tools that read or change other systems, letting it take several steps towards a goal, and adding stopping rules, approvals and logs turns it into an agent. Add one guarded action at a time.

Are AI agents better than chatbots?

Not in general. Agents suit tasks that need actions across systems. For answering questions, a chatbot is usually cheaper, faster and easier to test, and its mistakes are easier to contain.
