Approval workflows for AI agents in production

An approval workflow for AI agents in production needs five things decided before launch: a written policy saying which actions need sign-off and from whom, a review queue that routes each request to someone with authority and context, notifications that reach that person, timeouts that escalate and then expire without acting, and an audit log recording the proposal, the decision and the result. Review the workflow regularly so approval stays meaningful rather than routine.

Who this guide is for

This guide is for product owners, operations leads and buyers planning an AI agent that will act in real systems. Human-in-the-loop approval for AI agents covers the mechanics: how to classify actions, the kinds of human input and how to pause and resume a run. Here the focus is the organisational layer that the code depends on: ownership, routing, response times, escalation and records. These decisions are usually made by the team that owns the process, not by the developer.

Turn risk into a written approval policy

Start with a table that lists every action the agent can take, and for each one records who may approve it, under what conditions, and what happens if nobody does. Writing it down forces decisions that otherwise get made silently in code.

  • Name a role, not a person. "Finance lead on duty" survives holidays and staff changes; a named individual does not.
  • Use thresholds where they exist. Many organisations already have signing limits for refunds, discounts or purchases. Reuse them, so the agent follows the same rules as staff.
  • Separate the requester from the approver. If an employee asked the agent to act, someone else may need to approve, just as they would for a manual request.
  • Keep some actions off the list entirely. If an action should never be automated, remove the tool rather than relying on reviewers to refuse it.

The policy must be enforced in code that the model cannot change. AI agent security risks explains why: an agent that reads untrusted content can be steered into proposing harmful actions, and an approval rule written only in a prompt can be argued around.

Design the review queue

A queue is where approval succeeds or fails. Treat it as a product with users, not a database table.

  • Route by authority and context. Send each request to the team that owns the affected system, and to people allowed to approve that amount or data type.
  • Prioritise by urgency. A customer waiting in a live chat needs a faster decision than an overnight data clean-up. Show the deadline on each item.
  • Claim before deciding. When several people share a queue, let one person claim an item so two reviewers do not act on it at once, and release claims that sit untouched.
  • Show what matters. The proposed action, the evidence the agent used, what will change and how to undo it. If a reviewer has to open three other systems to decide, they will eventually stop doing so.

Every request should reach exactly one final state: approved, edited and approved, rejected, expired or cancelled. Requests that hang in an unclear state are where duplicate actions and lost work come from.

Notifications that get read

Notify people in the tool they already watch, whether that is a chat channel, email or a ticketing system, and link straight to the item. Keep the message short and leave the detail on the review page, where it can be shown securely and in full.

Be careful about approving from the notification itself. A one-click approve button in a chat message is convenient, but it invites approval without reading and may expose sensitive details in a channel with a wide audience. A reasonable compromise is to allow one-click approval only for low-impact tiers and require the full review page for the rest.

Group notifications where you can. A digest of routine items, with urgent ones sent individually, keeps the urgent ones noticeable.

Timeouts and escalation

Decide in advance what happens when nobody answers. A sensible default for most actions is a sequence: a reminder to the assigned reviewer, then escalation to a deputy or the owning team, then expiry. An expired request must not run. Tell the person or system waiting for the result that it expired, and give them a way to ask again.

Set the deadlines from the process, not from technology. A refund promised within a working day and a contract change reviewed weekly need very different clocks. Take working hours and holidays into account, and check that escalation never routes a request to someone without authority to decide.

Also decide what happens if the world changes while a request waits. If the customer cancelled, the record was edited or the price moved, the approved proposal may no longer be correct. Re-check the key facts at the moment of execution and send the item back for review if they have changed.

Audit logs that answer real questions

When something goes wrong, someone will ask what the agent proposed, who approved it, what they saw and what actually happened. Record enough to answer those questions without guesswork:

  • The proposal as the agent made it, and the version that was approved, if edited.
  • The policy rule that required approval, and the version of the policy in force.
  • Who reviewed it, when, their decision and any reason given.
  • Notifications, reminders, escalations and expiry, with timestamps.
  • The result of execution, including errors and any undo.

Make the log append-only, restrict who can read it, and apply your retention and data protection rules, since proposals often contain personal data. LLM observability and tracing covers linking these records to the agent's own traces.

Keeping approval meaningful

Approval fatigue is a known risk: people who approve many similar requests start approving them without reading. Rather than repeat the earlier guide's design advice, here is how to watch for it once the system is live:

  • Review the queue periodically. Look at how often each type of request is approved unchanged, how long decisions take and how often items expire. Very fast, unchanged approvals across a tier suggest the check is no longer adding much.
  • Change the policy, not the reviewers' habits. If a tier is consistently safe, consider automating it with logging and undo. If it is risky but routine, give the agent better constraints so fewer bad proposals reach people.
  • Sample decisions afterwards. A second person checking a small sample of approved actions shows whether approvals are careful, without slowing every request.
  • Listen to reviewers. Their complaints about missing context or repetitive items are usually the quickest route to a better workflow.

Questions to settle before launch

If you are commissioning an agent, ask for these answers in writing: the approval policy table, who owns the queue, the deadline and escalation path for each tier, what expiry does, what the audit log contains and who can read it, and how the policy will be reviewed after launch. Frequently asked questions

Which AI agent actions should need human approval?

Actions that are hard to reverse, reach customers or third parties, involve money, personal data or access rights, or affect many records at once. Write them into a policy table with an approver role and a deadline for each.

What should happen if nobody approves an AI agent's request?

Send a reminder, escalate to a deputy or the owning team, and then let the request expire without running. Tell whoever is waiting that it expired, so that they can ask again.

What should an audit log for agent approvals contain?

The original proposal, any edited version, the policy rule that applied, the reviewer, the decision and reason, notifications and escalations with timestamps, and the result of execution.

How do you avoid approval fatigue with AI agents?

Gate only actions that deserve attention, review the queue periodically for very fast unchanged approvals, automate tiers that prove safe, sample past decisions and act on reviewers' feedback.

Can reviewers approve agent actions from a chat notification?

It is possible, but it encourages approval without reading and can expose details in shared channels. Many teams limit one-click approval to low-impact actions and use a full review page for the rest.
