Working with a remote AI developer

Working with a remote AI developer goes well when you agree a few things at the start: a regular overlap time for calls, written updates in between, milestones you can try for yourself, code and accounts kept in your name, a handover plan, and clear rules on what data they can access. Time zones matter less than people expect once communication is mostly written and decisions are recorded.

Time zones: the arithmetic

As an example, take a developer based in India working with clients in the UK and the United States. India uses a single time zone, five and a half hours ahead of UTC, and does not change its clocks. The UK and US do change theirs, on different dates, so the gaps below move by an hour across the year. Treat them as general arithmetic, not a schedule:

  • UK. India is four and a half hours ahead during British Summer Time and five and a half hours ahead in winter. A UK morning falls in the Indian afternoon, so there is a natural shared block of working hours.
  • US East Coast. India is nine and a half or ten and a half hours ahead. An early morning in New York is evening in India, which gives a shorter window for calls.
  • US West Coast. India is twelve and a half or thirteen and a half hours ahead, so ordinary working days barely overlap. Calls usually need one side to start early or finish late, and most of the work runs on written updates.

A time difference has an upside: work can progress while you are offline, and you can find an update waiting in the morning. The cost is that a question asked at the wrong moment may wait a day for an answer. Good habits on both sides reduce that cost considerably.

Agree a regular call slot that suits both sides and put it in both calendars with time zones shown. Check it again for the weeks around clock changes, because the US and Europe change on different dates.

Communication that works across distance

  • Write things down. Decisions made on a call should be summarised in writing afterwards, by whoever is responsible for that. It avoids confusion and gives a record for later.
  • Regular written updates. A short note on what was done, what is next and what is blocked, at an agreed rhythm. You should never need to ask whether anything is happening.
  • Batch questions. Collect open questions in one place, such as a shared document or issue tracker, so they can be answered together rather than in scattered messages.
  • Calls for decisions, not status. Use video calls for things that benefit from discussion: reviewing a demonstration, settling a trade-off, agreeing scope.
  • One contact on your side. A named person who can answer questions and make decisions saves a lot of waiting.
  • Agree response times. For example, messages answered within one working day, and a separate route for anything urgent.

Milestones you can check yourself

With a remote developer, milestones replace the reassurance of seeing someone at work. Each milestone should end with something you can try, not only a report. For an AI system, that usually means a working version you can use on real examples, plus results against the test questions you agreed at the start. How to scope an AI project explains how to set that test up.

Keep the first milestone small and paid. It shows how the developer communicates, how they handle a surprise in your data and whether their updates match what you see. After that, agree each milestone's scope, deliverables and acceptance in writing before work starts.

Code ownership

Ownership should be settled before any work begins, not at the end:

  • Repository. Create the code repository in your organisation's account and invite the developer, rather than the other way round. You can then see progress at any time.
  • Cloud and provider accounts. Hosting, databases and model provider accounts should be in your name and paid by you, with the developer given the access they need.
  • Contract terms. State that the work produced for you, including code, prompts, test sets and documentation, belongs to you on payment, and note any open-source or reusable components the developer brings.
  • Secrets. Keys and passwords should be stored in a proper secrets manager, not pasted into chat or committed to code.

Data access and confidentiality

AI projects often involve sensitive documents or customer data, so be deliberate about it:

  • Sign a confidentiality agreement before sharing anything sensitive.
  • Share the least data that does the job. A cleaned or anonymised sample is often enough for early work. Grant wider access only when it is needed.
  • Prefer read-only and test access. Give access to test environments or read-only copies where possible, especially for systems that the AI will eventually act on.
  • Know where data goes. Ask which model and hosting providers will process your data, and check that this fits your contracts and any data protection rules that apply to you. Data crossing borders may matter for your compliance team.
  • Revoke access at the end. Keep a list of every access you grant and remove it when the engagement finishes.

If different users should see different documents in the finished system, that needs designing in from the start; RAG access control and permissions explains how.

The first week

A little setup at the start prevents most of the friction later. In the first week, aim to have the repository and accounts created on your side, a shared place for questions and decisions, the regular call slot in both calendars, a confidentiality agreement signed and the first milestone written down with what you will receive and how you will judge it. Introduce the developer to anyone they will need answers from, such as the person who knows the documents or the IT contact who grants access. It is also worth agreeing public holidays on both sides, since they differ between countries and can otherwise surprise you mid-milestone.

Handover

A good handover means someone else could take over the system. Ask for:

  • Setup and deployment instructions that have been tested by someone other than the author.
  • The prompts, test set and how to run the tests.
  • A short explanation of the design and the main decisions behind it.
  • Notes on running costs, monitoring and known limitations.
  • A recorded walkthrough call for whoever will maintain it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I work with an AI developer in a different time zone?

Yes. Agree a regular call slot in the overlapping hours, rely on written updates and recorded decisions in between, and check the slot around clock changes. Work can then progress while you are offline.

What are the overlapping working hours between India and the UK or US?

India is four and a half or five and a half hours ahead of the UK, so a UK morning overlaps the Indian afternoon. US East Coast mornings overlap Indian evenings, while US West Coast working days barely overlap.

How do I protect my code and data with a remote developer?

Keep the repository, cloud and model provider accounts in your name, sign a confidentiality agreement, share the least data needed, prefer read-only or test access, and revoke all access when the engagement ends.

What should a remote AI developer hand over at the end?

Code in your repository, tested setup and deployment instructions, prompts and test sets, a short design explanation, notes on costs, monitoring and limitations, and a recorded walkthrough for whoever will maintain the system.
