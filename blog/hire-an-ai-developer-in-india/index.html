How to hire an AI developer in India

To hire an AI developer in India, write a short brief with real examples of the problem, look for evidence of working systems rather than buzzwords, start with a small paid first milestone, and put scope, payment, invoicing and code ownership in a written contract before work begins. The same steps work whether you are an Indian startup, an established Indian business or an overseas company considering India.

Who this guide is for

This guide is for two groups. The first is Indian founders and business owners who want an AI feature built, such as a chatbot over company documents, an automated document workflow or an agent that works with internal tools, and who are deciding between a freelancer, an agency and an in-house hire. The second is companies outside India who are considering working with a developer here.

The advice is mostly the same for both. The differences are practical: time zones, payment routes and paperwork. I am a freelance AI/ML engineer based in India, so read this with that in mind; the checks below apply to anyone you consider, me included. For a deeper look at judging technical skill, read hire an AI engineer: what to look for.

Work out what you need before you look

"AI developer" covers very different work. Before you speak to anyone, decide which of these is closest to your project:

  • A model-powered feature in an existing product. For example, summarising tickets or drafting replies. This is mostly backend work with careful prompt design and testing.
  • Answers from your own documents. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over policies, manuals or product data, usually as a chatbot or search box.
  • Agents and workflows. A system that takes several steps, calls your tools and sometimes waits for a person to approve an action.
  • Language processing on your data. Classifying, extracting or tagging text at volume, an NLP task where a smaller model may beat a large one on cost.
  • A full product. Frontend, backend, database and deployment around the AI part.

Knowing which one you need tells you what experience to look for and stops you paying for a research project when you wanted a working feature.

What to look for in a developer

Look for evidence that the person has built something that works and can explain the trade-offs:

  • Working examples. A project you can see, a code repository, or a demonstration they can walk you through. Ask what went wrong and how they fixed it.
  • Honest scoping. A good developer asks about your data, your users and how you will judge success before talking about models. Be wary of anyone who promises a fixed accuracy before seeing your documents.
  • Testing habits. Ask how they would know the system is answering well. You want to hear about a set of test questions, checks on real examples and a way to catch regressions.
  • Full stack ability, if you need it. Many AI projects struggle on the plumbing: authentication, deployment, logging and cost control. Check who will handle those.
  • Clear writing. Most remote work runs on written updates, so the quality of their first few messages is a useful signal.

How to brief an AI developer

A good brief saves days of back and forth. It does not need to be long. One or two pages covering the following is enough:

  • The problem in plain words. Who is affected, what they do today and what should change.
  • Real examples. Ten to twenty genuine questions, documents or cases, with the answers you would expect. Remove personal data first.
  • Where the data lives. Google Drive, a database, a CRM, PDFs on a shared drive, and roughly how much there is.
  • Who will use it. Staff, customers, or both, and in which languages. If users mix Hindi and English, say so early.
  • Constraints. Data that must stay in a certain place, systems it must connect to, and any budget or deadline you already have.
  • What done looks like. How you will decide the system is good enough to launch.

How to scope an AI project goes further on turning that brief into milestones and acceptance tests.

Start with a small paid first milestone

The best way to judge a developer is to work with them on something small and real. Agree a first milestone of limited size, pay for it, and make it end in something you can try yourself, such as a working prototype on a sample of your data with results against your test questions.

A paid milestone is fairer than an unpaid test task and tells you more. You see how the developer communicates, how they handle the surprises every real dataset contains, and whether their updates match what you see when you try the result. If it goes well, you continue; if not, you have lost little and you still own what was produced.

Agree in writing what the milestone includes, what it does not, the date, the price and how you will accept it. Then do the same for each later milestone.

Contracts and invoicing basics

Even a small project deserves a written agreement. At minimum it should cover:

  • Scope and milestones. What will be delivered, in what order, and how each part is accepted.
  • Payment terms. Amounts per milestone, when invoices are raised and when they are due.
  • Change requests. How new ideas are estimated and approved, so the scope does not drift quietly.
  • Confidentiality. What the developer may and may not do with your data and documents.
  • Ownership. Covered in the next section, but it belongs in the contract.
  • Ending the agreement. Notice, payment for work done and handover of everything produced.

Ask for a proper invoice for each payment, showing the work covered and the agreed amount. Tax treatment, including GST and anything that applies to payments from abroad, depends on your circumstances and the developer's, so check with your accountant before you agree the payment terms. Overseas clients should also ask their accountant how they will pay and what records they need to keep.

IP and code ownership

Settle ownership at the start, not at handover:

  • Your repository. Create the code repository in your own account and invite the developer.
  • Your accounts. Hosting, databases and model provider accounts should be in your name and billed to you, with the developer given the access they need and no more.
  • Clear assignment. The contract should say that the work produced for you, including code, prompts, test sets and documentation, belongs to you once paid for.
  • Existing components. Developers often reuse their own utilities or open-source libraries. That is normal, but the contract should say which parts are reused and on what licence you can use them.
  • Secrets. API keys belong in a secrets manager, never in chat messages or committed to code.

If the project involves customer or employee data, agree how it will be accessed and stored, and remove access when the work ends. A lawyer can tailor the contract to your situation; the points above are what to raise with them.

Communication that keeps a project on track

Most problems in freelance projects are communication problems. A few habits prevent most of them:

  • Agree the channel. Many Indian businesses prefer WhatsApp for quick messages, while others use email or a shared tracker. Pick one place for decisions so nothing gets lost, and write down anything agreed on a call.
  • Regular updates. A short note at an agreed rhythm covering what was done, what is next and what is blocked.
  • One decision maker on your side. A named person who can answer questions quickly.
  • Demonstrations over reports. Each milestone should end with something you can use, not only a description.

Overseas clients should also plan around the time difference. India does not change its clocks, so the overlap with Europe and the US shifts during the year. Working with a remote AI developer covers time zones, handover and data access in more detail.

Freelancer, agency or in-house?

A freelancer suits a well-defined project where you want to talk directly to the person building it. An agency suits work that needs several roles at once, such as design, project management and a team of developers. An in-house hire suits AI work that will be central to your product for years. Frequently asked questions

How do I find a good AI developer in India?

Write a short brief with real examples, then look for developers who can show working systems and explain trade-offs. Talk to a few, ask how they would test the result, and start with a small paid milestone.

Should the first task be paid?

Yes. A small paid first milestone is fairer than an unpaid test and tells you more, because you see real work on your own data, how the developer communicates and whether the result matches their updates.

Who owns the code when I hire a freelance AI developer?

Agree it in the contract before work starts. A common arrangement is that the work produced for you belongs to you once paid for, with the code kept in a repository in your own account.

Do I need to pay GST when hiring a freelancer in India?

It depends on your situation and the developer's, including where each of you is based. Check with your accountant before agreeing payment terms, and ask for a proper invoice for every payment.

Can overseas companies work with an AI developer in India?

Yes. Plan for the time difference, agree a regular call slot and written updates, keep code and accounts in your own name, and ask your accountant about payments and records.
