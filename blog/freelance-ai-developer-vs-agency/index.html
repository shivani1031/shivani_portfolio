Freelance AI developer vs agency: which should you hire?

A freelance AI developer usually suits a bounded project where you want direct contact with the person building it and can accept that cover depends on one person. An agency is often the better choice when the build needs several specialists at once, has to run for a long time with guaranteed cover, or needs formal processes your organisation requires. Whichever you choose, confirm who does the work and make sure you own the result.

Start with the shape of the work

The choice is less about which option is better in general and more about which fits your project. Before comparing anyone, answer three questions:

  • How many different skills does the build need at the same time? An internal document assistant may need one person who can handle the AI work, a backend and a simple interface. A customer-facing product with mobile apps, a design system and compliance reviews needs several people working in parallel.
  • How long will it run, and how much does it matter if work pauses? A project that can wait a week while someone is unwell is different from one with a public launch date.
  • What does your organisation require from suppliers? Some companies need formal security questionnaires, specific insurance, framework agreements or a supplier above a certain size.

If you have not yet written these down, how to scope an AI project will help, and the answers often make the choice obvious.

Where a freelancer tends to fit

  • You speak to the person building it. Questions get answered by the one who wrote the code, without a project manager relaying them. For AI work, where much depends on judgement about your data and acceptable errors, that directness is useful.
  • The scope is bounded. A proof of concept, a first version of one feature, a RAG assistant over a defined set of documents or an automation for one workflow.
  • Less overhead. There are fewer layers of account management, status meetings and internal handoffs, so more of what you pay for goes into the build itself. Whether that makes the total cheaper depends on the individual and the project, so compare actual proposals.
  • Flexibility. It is often easier to agree a small paid first milestone, adjust scope as you learn and stop cleanly at a natural point.

The risks are real too. One person can be ill, busy or unavailable. One person may be strong in AI and weaker in, say, frontend design or infrastructure. And quality depends almost entirely on that individual, so you have to judge them well. Hiring an AI engineer: what to look for covers how.

Where an agency is the better choice

It is worth being plain about this: for some projects, an agency is the right answer.

  • Several specialists working at once. If you need AI engineering, mobile development, design, quality assurance and infrastructure in parallel, an agency can staff that and coordinate it. Assembling and managing several freelancers yourself is real work.
  • Cover and continuity. An agency can replace someone who leaves or falls ill, and can usually keep a team on a long programme of work.
  • Formal requirements. Larger organisations may need security certifications, insurance levels, support contracts with response times or procurement processes that suit a company better than an individual.
  • Round-the-clock support. If the system needs someone available at any hour after launch, a team with a rota can offer that more realistically.

The common agency risks are also worth knowing. The senior person in the sales meeting may not be the one who builds your system. Work can be passed between staff, losing context. And AI may be one of many services rather than the agency's depth, so check who has actually built systems like yours. Ask to meet the people who will do the work.

Questions that apply to both

  • "Who exactly will write the code, and can we speak to them?"
  • "What do you need to see before giving an estimate?"
  • "How will we both know that it works?" Look for an agreed test set rather than a demonstration.
  • "What happens if the person working on this becomes unavailable?"
  • "Whose accounts will the code, cloud resources and model provider keys live in?"
  • "What will we receive at handover, and could another developer pick it up?"
  • "How will running costs be monitored after launch?" Controlling LLM cost and latency explains what drives them.

Reducing the risk either way

Most of the protections are the same whoever you hire:

  • Keep ownership on your side. The code repository, cloud account, domain and model provider account should be in your organisation's name from the beginning, with the supplier given access.
  • Start with a small paid milestone. A short, fixed piece of work on your real data shows how a supplier communicates and handles surprises before you commit to more.
  • Agree what "done" means in writing. Include the test set, the documentation and the handover session.
  • Insist on documentation a stranger could use. This matters most with a freelancer, but agencies change staff too.
  • Name an owner on your side. Someone in your organisation should make decisions, review milestones and own the system after launch.

If you go with a freelancer working remotely, working with a remote AI developer covers time zones, milestones and handover in more detail.

Comparing proposals side by side

Proposals from a freelancer and an agency often look very different, which makes them hard to compare. Put them on the same footing by checking each one for the same items:

  • Named people. Who will do the work, and what share of their time it gets.
  • Discovery. Whether there is a step to examine your data before the main estimate, and what it produces.
  • Testing. How quality will be measured, and against what agreed examples.
  • Milestones. What you can try at the end of each one, and how you approve it.
  • Ownership and handover. What you receive and in whose accounts it lives.
  • After launch. What support is included, for how long and how quickly issues are answered.
  • Exclusions. What is not included, which is often where the real differences are.

A proposal that is vague on several of these is harder to hold anyone to, whatever the size of the supplier.

A middle path

The choice does not have to be all or nothing. A common pattern is to use a freelancer for discovery and a first version, then decide on the long-term arrangement once you know what the system needs. Another is to bring in a freelance specialist for the AI part alongside your own developers or an existing agency. If you do this, make one person responsible for how the pieces fit together, because gaps between suppliers are where projects tend to stall.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to hire a freelancer or an agency for AI development?

It depends on the project. A freelancer often suits a bounded build where you want direct contact with the developer. An agency is often better when you need several specialists at once, guaranteed cover or formal supplier processes.

Is a freelance AI developer cheaper than an agency?

Not necessarily. A freelancer has fewer layers of overhead, but the total depends on the individual, the scope and how much coordination you take on yourself. Compare written proposals for the same scope rather than assuming.

What are the risks of hiring a freelance AI developer?

The main risks are reliance on one person, gaps in skills outside their specialism and quality that depends entirely on that individual. Reduce them with a paid first milestone, good documentation and code kept in your accounts.

When should I choose an agency for an AI project?

Choose an agency when the build needs several specialists in parallel, must run for a long time with cover for absence, needs support at any hour, or when your organisation requires certifications, insurance or procurement terms an individual cannot meet.
