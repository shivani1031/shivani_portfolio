Conversational AI vs chatbots: what differs and how to choose

A chatbot is any software that converses through a chat interface, often following scripted flows. Conversational AI uses language models to understand free-form language and respond flexibly. Choose scripted flows when the tasks are few and answers must be exact, conversational AI when requests vary widely, and often a mix of both.

The terms overlap

These words are not used consistently, and vendors use them loosely, so it helps to set the labels aside. "Chatbot" describes an interface: something you talk to through messages. "Conversational AI" describes a family of techniques behind such an interface. A product can be both, and some "AI chatbots" contain very little AI.

The useful question is what decides each reply. There are broadly three answers, and most real products sit on a spectrum between them.

Three designs behind a chat window

Scripted, rule-based bots

The designer writes a decision tree: buttons, keywords and fixed replies. The bot can say only what has been written. That makes it predictable, easy to audit and cheap to run. It also breaks the moment someone goes off-script, and the tree can become hard to maintain as branches accumulate.

Intent-based NLP bots

A trained classifier maps each message to a known intent, such as "track order", and often pulls out details such as an order number. Replies and flows are still authored by people. This copes with different wordings of a known request, but it needs training examples for each intent and will struggle with anything outside the list.

LLM-based assistants

A language model interprets the message in context and generates the reply, usually with instructions, and often with retrieved documents or tools. It handles phrasing nobody anticipated, follow-up questions and changes of subject. In exchange, its output is not fully predictable and may be wrong while sounding confident, so it needs testing and guardrails that a script never did.

The most important row is the failure mode. A scripted bot fails visibly: the customer hits a dead end and is annoyed. An LLM-based assistant can fail invisibly: the customer receives a fluent and incorrect answer and acts on it. Which of these you can tolerate should weigh heavily in your decision.

Memory and context

Handling context is what makes a conversation feel natural. In a scripted bot, context is explicit state: the flow knows which step it has reached and what was collected. In an LLM-based assistant, earlier turns are normally sent with each request, so "what about the second one?" can be understood. That flexibility has a cost, as histories grow and must be trimmed or summarised, and details can be lost in the process. Agent memory explains the options for what to keep.

Whichever design you choose, values that matter, such as a verified customer ID or an order total, should be held in application state rather than left for a model to remember from the transcript.

When a scripted bot is the better choice

  • There are few tasks, and they are well defined. Booking a slot, checking an order status or resetting a password may need nothing more than a short flow or a good form.
  • The wording must be exact. Regulated statements or legal disclosures should be presented as approved, and not paraphrased by a model.
  • Each step must be auditable. A fixed path is easier to review and to show that it was followed.
  • There is little content or budget for upkeep. An LLM with nothing reliable to draw on tends to produce confident generalities.

Also ask whether conversation is the right interface. Users often prefer a clear menu, a search box or a form for a simple task, as typing a sentence can be slower than one click. Avoid building a chat experience merely because chat is fashionable.

When conversational AI earns its place

  • Requests vary widely. Your logs show many different wordings and combinations that no reasonable tree could cover.
  • The answers exist in a large body of content. Policies and documentation can be retrieved and summarised for each question. What RAG is and when to use it explains the pattern, and RAG chatbot vs custom GPT looks at how to build it.
  • Follow-up questions are normal. People need to refine, compare and clarify over several turns.
  • Users mix several requests. One message such as "change my delivery address and tell me when the refund arrives" is hard for a tree but workable for a model with suitable tools.
  • A mistake can be caught or corrected. The answer has citations, staff check it, or the stakes are modest.

Most good systems are hybrids

This is rarely an either-or decision. A common structure uses a model to understand the message, then ordinary code to carry out the sensitive parts:

  • Route, then follow a flow. A model or classifier identifies the request, and a tested flow handles anything involving money, identity or account changes.
  • Generate within limits. Questions about general content get generated answers with citations from approved sources.
  • Fix some replies. Legal statements and complaint procedures come from approved text, whatever the model might otherwise say.
  • Use deterministic checks. Code, not the model, confirms that the customer is verified before a refund is issued.
  • Hand off. A person takes over when asked, when confidence is low or when the topic is sensitive, ideally with the conversation so far attached.

If the assistant goes on to plan steps and take actions on its own, it is moving into agent territory, which brings different questions. What is agentic AI? explains that boundary.

How to choose: six questions

  1. What do people really ask? Read real chat logs, support tickets or emails. If a few requests make up most of the volume, flows may cover much of it. A long tail of variety favours a model.
  2. What does a wrong answer cost? If a wrong answer could cause financial, legal or safety harm, keep that part scripted or reviewed by a person.
  3. Is the content ready? An LLM-based assistant is limited by its sources. Outdated or conflicting documents will produce outdated or conflicting answers.
  4. Does it need to act? Answering questions and changing records carry very different risks. Actions need authenticated tools with permissions enforced in code.
  5. Who will maintain it? Scripts need editing, and models need content upkeep, evaluation and monitoring. Someone must own this after launch.
  6. Can you measure success? Choose outcomes such as resolved requests and correct answers. Number of conversations alone can be misleading, as a confused user generates more messages.

Questions to ask about a product

When a supplier describes something as conversational AI, these questions cut through the label:

  • What happens when it does not know the answer?
  • Can it show the source of each statement?
  • Which actions can it take, and who authorises them?
  • How does it hand off to a person, and what is passed along?
  • How is it tested, and on whose data?
  • Can we review conversations, and how long are they retained?

Testing before launch

Scripts can be tested by walking every path. Generated answers cannot be covered that way, because the same question may be answered differently and the possible inputs are endless. Use a set of realistic conversations with expected outcomes, including off-topic questions and attempts to misuse the system, then review samples after launch. Also check for tone, since an assistant that apologises at length and solves nothing is not an improvement on a dead end.

Where to start

Start with your most common requests and sort them into three groups: those that suit a fixed flow, those that can be answered from content and those that need a person. That sorting usually reveals the design. Launch one group, measure it and then expand. If you are weighing up an LLM-based assistant, see generative AI and LLM development.

Frequently asked questions

Is ChatGPT a chatbot or conversational AI?

Both labels can apply. It has a chat interface, which makes it a chatbot in the broad sense, and it uses a language model to interpret and generate language, which is what conversational AI usually refers to.

Are rule-based chatbots obsolete?

No. For a few well-defined tasks, or where wording must be exact, fixed flows remain predictable, auditable and inexpensive. Many LLM-based products also keep scripted paths for their sensitive steps.

Does conversational AI need RAG?

Only if it must answer from your own content. An assistant that collects details or calls tools may not need it. For questions about your policies or documentation, retrieval is a common way to ground answers.

Can a scripted bot be upgraded to conversational AI?

Often, and gradually. Keep the flows that work, add a model to interpret messages and route them to those flows, then add generated answers for informational questions. Test each step against real conversations.
