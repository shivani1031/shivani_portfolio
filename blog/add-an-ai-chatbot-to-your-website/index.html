Adding an AI chatbot to your website: a buyer's guide

To add an AI chatbot that answers from your own content, choose between a hosted chatbot tool, a feature of your existing help desk or a custom build, then prepare clean source content, a list of real questions and a plan for handing over to a person. The right option depends on how much control, privacy and integration you need.

What this kind of chatbot does

A chatbot that answers from your own content works by searching your material when a visitor asks a question, then having a language model write an answer from the passages it found. The technical name for this is retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, and what RAG is and when to use it explains it in more detail.

The important consequence for a buyer is that the chatbot can only be as good as the content behind it. If your pages are out of date, contradict each other or leave the common questions unanswered, the chatbot will reflect that, often with more confidence than the pages themselves.

This guide is for business owners and managers deciding how to proceed. If your main aim is handling customer support tickets, AI chatbots for customer support covers that case specifically.

Your main options

A hosted chatbot tool

Many services let you upload documents or point at your website, then paste a snippet of code into your pages to show a chat window. Setup is usually quick and needs little technical skill.

The trade-off is control. The vendor decides how your content is split, searched and ranked, and you may not be able to see why a wrong answer was given. Check where your content and visitors' messages are stored, whether they are used to train models, and what happens to your data if you leave.

A feature of software you already use

Help desk, website and e-commerce platforms increasingly include an AI assistant. If yours does, it may already have access to your help articles and be able to hand a conversation to your team in the same system. The limits are similar to a hosted tool, and it usually works best with content that already lives in that platform.

A custom build

A developer builds the retrieval pipeline, the chat interface and the integrations to your requirements. This gives you control over which sources are used, how answers cite them, how access is restricted, how conversations are logged and which model is used. It also means more decisions, a system someone must maintain, and testing that you cannot skip.

A custom build tends to make sense when content is spread across several systems, when answers must respect who is asking, when you need to connect to live data such as orders, or when your data cannot go to a general-purpose vendor. The comparison in RAG chatbot vs custom GPT goes into these trade-offs.

What to prepare before you start

Whichever option you choose, the preparation is largely the same, and it has more effect on quality than the choice of tool.

  • A list of real questions. Collect the questions people actually ask, from your inbox, contact forms, calls and staff. Note the correct answer for each. This list becomes your test.
  • An inventory of content. List the pages and documents that answer those questions, who owns each one and when it was last reviewed. Remove or update anything out of date before the chatbot can repeat it.
  • Clear gaps. Where a common question has no written answer, write one. A chatbot cannot answer from content that does not exist.
  • A scope. Decide which topics the chatbot should answer and which it should decline, such as legal, medical or pricing questions that need a person.
  • A handover plan. Decide what happens when the chatbot cannot help: a contact form, an email address, opening hours for live chat or a callback request.
  • A tone. Agree how it should sound, how it introduces itself and that it says clearly it is an AI assistant.

The risks to plan for

Wrong answers stated confidently

Even with good content, a chatbot can misread a passage, combine two sources wrongly or answer beyond what the content says. Reduce this by instructing it to answer only from retrieved content, showing links to its sources, and declining when nothing relevant is found. Then test it, because instructions alone are not a guarantee.

Commitments you did not intend

Visitors may treat what the chatbot says as your company's position. Keep it away from promises about refunds, prices, delivery or eligibility unless the answer comes straight from a current, approved source, and take advice on your own obligations where answers carry legal weight.

Manipulation

People will try to make a public chatbot say things it should not, or reveal its instructions. If it can take actions or see private data, the risk is greater. Limit what it can access and do, and see LLM guardrails and prompt injection defence for the main defences.

Privacy

Visitors type personal details into chat windows, whether asked to or not. Know where conversations are stored, for how long, who can read them and which third parties process them, and update your privacy notice to match.

Stale content

A chatbot built once and left alone drifts out of date as your business changes. Someone needs to own the content and the index, with a process for updating both.

Running costs

Costs often depend on usage, so they can rise with traffic or with abuse. Ask how usage is charged and what limits can be set.

Questions to ask a developer or vendor

  • Which of our sources will it use, and how is new or changed content picked up?
  • Will answers show which page or document they came from?
  • What does it do when our content does not answer the question?
  • How will you test it before launch, and can we see the results on our own questions?
  • Where are conversations and our content stored, and are they used to train any model?
  • How does a visitor reach a person, and does the conversation go with them?
  • What can we change ourselves, such as content, scope and wording, without a developer?
  • How are costs calculated, and how can usage be capped?
  • What happens to our data and setup if we stop using the service?
  • How will we see what visitors ask and which answers failed?

Good answers are specific. Be wary of claims that the chatbot will never make mistakes or will need no maintenance; neither is true of any system of this kind. Hiring an AI engineer: what to look for has further advice on assessing a developer.

A sensible way to begin

Start narrow. Pick one area with clear, well-maintained content and a steady stream of questions, such as delivery and returns. Test the chatbot privately against your question list, fix the content gaps it exposes, then release it to visitors with an obvious route to a person. Review the conversations regularly, especially those where it declined or a visitor asked for help, and widen the scope only when the first area works well.

Frequently asked questions

Can an AI chatbot answer only from my own website content?

It can be built to search your content and instruct the model to answer only from what it finds, declining otherwise. This reduces off-topic and invented answers but does not remove them, so testing on your own questions is still needed.

Do I need a custom build or will a hosted tool do?

A hosted tool can suit a small, public knowledge base. A custom build makes more sense when content is spread across systems, answers depend on who is asking, live data is involved or your data cannot go to a general-purpose vendor.

What should I prepare before adding a chatbot?

Prepare a list of real questions with correct answers, an inventory of up-to-date content, a decision on what the chatbot should not answer and a plan for handing visitors to a person.

Will the chatbot keep itself up to date?

Not by itself. Someone needs to own the source content and make sure changes reach the chatbot's index, and to review conversations for questions it answers badly.
