How to hire a RAG developer

To hire a RAG developer, start with a clear problem and a sample of your real documents, then judge candidates on how they plan to test answer quality, handle messy files and control who sees what. Ask how they would measure retrieval, what they would build first and what you will own at the end. A small paid first milestone tells you more than any demonstration.

What a RAG project involves

RAG, or retrieval-augmented generation, lets a language model answer questions from your own documents. The system searches your content for passages relevant to a question, then asks the model to answer using only those passages and to cite them. If you are not yet sure it is the right approach, what RAG is and when to use it covers that decision first.

The chat window is the smallest part of the work. A typical project includes:

  • Getting the content in. Collecting documents from where they live, extracting text from PDFs, scans, spreadsheets or web pages, and keeping it up to date when the originals change.
  • Preparing it for search. Splitting documents into passages, attaching useful details such as titles, dates and owners, and choosing how to search them.
  • Answering. Writing the instructions the model follows, including what to do when the documents do not contain an answer.
  • Testing. Building a set of real questions with known good answers, and measuring the system against it whenever something changes.
  • Fitting it into your business. Sign-in, permissions, logging, an interface people will actually use and a way to report bad answers.

When you compare proposals, check that each of these is covered. A quote that describes only the chatbot has probably left out the parts that take the most effort.

What to prepare before you contact anyone

You do not need a technical specification. You need enough for a developer to see the real problem, and preparing it will make every conversation shorter and every estimate more grounded.

  • The problem in one paragraph. Who asks the questions today, who answers them and what goes wrong. How to scope an AI project gives a fuller set of questions if you want them.
  • A representative sample of documents. Include the awkward ones: scanned forms, long tables, old versions and anything people complain about. A developer shown only tidy files will underestimate the work.
  • Twenty or so real questions. Write down questions people actually ask, with the answer an expert would give and where it comes from. This becomes the start of your test set.
  • Who may see what. If some documents are restricted to certain teams or customers, say so now. It shapes the design from the beginning.
  • Where the data may go. Check whether your content may be sent to an external model provider, and whether any contracts or regulations apply.

Before sharing anything sensitive, ask for a confidentiality agreement and share a cleaned sample if you can.

How to judge a RAG developer

Hiring an AI engineer: what to look for covers the general signals, such as honest limits and inspectable work. For RAG in particular, listen for these:

  • They ask for your documents early. Retrieval quality depends heavily on the content, so a good developer wants to see it before promising anything.
  • They separate search problems from answer problems. When an answer is wrong, the right passage was either not found or was found and misused. Someone who can explain how they would tell the difference has debugged real systems.
  • They talk about measurement without being asked. Expect a plan for a test set and separate checks for retrieval and for answers. How to evaluate a RAG system shows what a sensible plan looks like.
  • They plan for "I don't know". A good system declines when the documents do not support an answer, rather than guessing.
  • They treat permissions as code. Access must be enforced when searching, not by telling the model to keep secrets. RAG access control and permissions explains why.
  • They can name what not to build. Sometimes better search or a cleaned-up help centre solves most of the problem, and a good developer will say so.

Questions to ask

  • "What would you need to see from us before giving an estimate?"
  • "Which of our documents worry you, and why?"
  • "How will we both know the answers are good enough? What will you measure?"
  • "What happens when a question cannot be answered from our content?"
  • "How will the system stay current when documents are added, changed or removed?"
  • "Which model and hosting providers would see our data, and can we choose alternatives?"
  • "What will running costs depend on, and how will we see them?"
  • "What do we receive at the end: code, prompts, test questions, deployment notes?"

Specific answers that refer back to your documents and your questions are a good sign. General reassurance, or a firm price before anyone has seen your content, is not.

How a remote engagement usually runs

Most RAG work can be done remotely over email, shared documents and video calls. The shape tends to be similar whoever you hire:

  1. Brief and sample. You share the problem, documents and questions described above, under a confidentiality agreement if needed.
  2. Paid discovery. A short, fixed piece of work in which the developer loads a slice of your content, tries your questions and reports what works, what does not and what the full build involves. This is the cheapest point to find out that the data is harder than expected.
  3. Agreed test questions. Before the main build, you agree the question set and what counts as a good answer. This becomes the shared definition of "working".
  4. Build in milestones. Each milestone ends with something you can try and a report against the test questions. You review, give feedback and approve the next step.
  5. Handover. Code in a repository you control, deployment instructions, the test set and a walkthrough for whoever will maintain it, followed by an agreed support period if you want one.

Each stage is a natural place to stop or change course. If a developer resists that structure, ask why. Working across time zones is covered in more detail in working with a remote AI developer.

Warning signs

  • A promise of a specific accuracy figure before seeing your documents.
  • No mention of testing, or testing described only as "we will try it".
  • Permissions handled by an instruction in the prompt.
  • Code, accounts or data stores that stay in the developer's name after the project.
  • A demonstration built on public documents presented as evidence it will work on yours.

After launch

A RAG system is never quite finished. Documents are added and retired, users ask questions nobody anticipated and model providers update or withdraw versions. Before you sign, agree who will review poorly rated answers, who keeps the content current and how the test questions will be rerun after any change. If that person is on your team, make sure the handover prepares them for it.

Frequently asked questions

What does a RAG developer do?

A RAG developer builds systems that answer questions from your own documents. The work covers loading and preparing content, search, the instructions the model follows, testing answer quality, permissions and fitting the system into your existing tools.

What should I prepare before hiring a RAG developer?

Prepare a short description of the problem, a representative sample of documents including the awkward ones, a list of real questions with expert answers, and notes on who may see which documents and where your data may be sent.

How do I know if a RAG developer is any good?

Look for someone who asks to see your documents early, plans how to measure retrieval and answers separately, designs for questions the documents cannot answer and enforces permissions in code rather than in the prompt.

Can a RAG project be done fully remotely?

Yes. Most RAG work runs well over email, shared documents and video calls, provided you agree milestones, share a sample of data securely and keep the code and accounts in your own name.
